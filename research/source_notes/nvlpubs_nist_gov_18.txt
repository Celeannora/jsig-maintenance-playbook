- **Formal incident response capability / operating structure**
  - Organizations must create, provision, and operate a formal incident response capability.
  - Federal law requires Federal agencies to report incidents to the United States Computer Emergency Readiness Team (US-CERT) office within the Department of Homeland Security (DHS).
  - FISMA requires Federal agencies to establish incident response capabilities.
  - Each Federal civilian agency must designate a primary and secondary point of contact (POC) with US-CERT and report all incidents consistent with the agency’s incident response policy.
  - Establishing an incident response capability should include:
    - Creating an incident response policy and plan
    - Developing procedures for performing incident handling and reporting
    - Setting guidelines for communicating with outside parties regarding incidents
    - Selecting a team structure and staffing model
    - Establishing relationships and lines of communication between the incident response team and other groups, both internal (e.g., legal department) and external (e.g., law enforcement agencies)
    - Determining what services the incident response team should provide
    - Staffing and training the incident response team

- **Roles / responsibilities / dependencies**
  - **Policy Elements**
    - Statement of management commitment
    - Purpose and objectives of the policy
    - Scope of the policy (to whom and what it applies and under what circumstances)
    - Definition of computer security incidents and related terms
    - Organizational structure and definition of roles, responsibilities, and levels of authority
      - Should include:
        - Authority of the incident response team to confiscate or disconnect equipment
        - Authority to monitor suspicious activity
        - Requirements for reporting certain types of incidents
        - Requirements and guidelines for external communications and information sharing (e.g., what can be shared with whom, when, and over what channels)
        - Handoff and escalation points in the incident management process
    - Prioritization or severity ratings of incidents
    - Performance measures
    - Reporting and contact forms
  - **Plan Elements**
    - Mission
    - Strategies and goals
    - Senior management approval
    - Organizational approach to incident response
    - How the incident response team will communicate with the rest of the organization and with other organizations
    - Metrics for measuring the incident response capability and its effectiveness
    - Roadmap for maturing the incident response capability
    - How the program fits into the overall organization
    - Review the plan at least annually
  - **Procedure Elements / SOPs**
    - Standard operating procedures (SOPs) are a delineation of the specific technical processes, techniques, checklists, and forms used by the incident response team.
    - SOPs should be reasonably comprehensive and detailed.
    - SOPs should be tested to validate their accuracy and usefulness, then distributed to all team members.
    - Training should be provided for SOP users.
  - **Incident response personnel**
    - A single employee, with one or more designated alternates, should be in charge of incident response.
    - In a fully outsourced model, this person oversees and evaluates the outsourcer’s work.
    - All other models generally have a team manager and one or more deputies who assumes authority in the absence of the team manager.
    - Managers:
      - Act as a liaison with upper management and other teams and organizations
      - Defuse crisis situations
      - Ensure that the team has the necessary personnel, resources, and skills
      - Are technically adept and have excellent communication skills
      - Are ultimately responsible for ensuring that incident response activities are performed properly
    - Technical lead:
      - Person with strong technical skills and incident response experience
      - Assumes oversight of and final responsibility for the quality of the team’s technical work
    - Incident lead:
      - Larger teams often assign an incident lead as the primary POC for handling a specific incident
      - Held accountable for the incident’s handling
      - May coordinate handlers’ activities, gather information from handlers, provide incident updates to other groups, and ensure that the team’s needs are met
    - Team member skills:
      - System administration
      - Network administration
      - Programming
      - Technical support
      - Intrusion detection
      - Problem solving skills
      - Critical thinking abilities
      - Teamwork skills
      - Communication skills
      - Speaking skills
      - Writing skills
  - **Dependencies within organizations**
    - Management
      - Establishes incident response policy, budget, and staffing
      - Ultimately responsible for coordinating incident response among various stakeholders, minimizing damage, and reporting to Congress, OMB, the General Accounting Office (GAO), and other parties
    - Information Assurance
      - May be needed during prevention, containment, eradication, and recovery
      - Example: altering network security controls (e.g., firewall rulesets)
    - IT Support
      - Technical experts such as system and network administrators
      - Assist and usually have the best understanding of the technology they manage
      - Can ensure appropriate actions are taken for the affected system, such as whether to disconnect an attacked system
    - Legal Department
      - Review incident response plans, policies, and procedures to ensure compliance with law and Federal guidance, including the right to privacy
      - Guidance sought if incident may have legal ramifications, including evidence collection, prosecution of a suspect, or a lawsuit
      - Guidance sought if there may be a need for a memorandum of understanding (MOU) or other binding agreements involving liability limitations for information sharing
    - Public Affairs and Media Relations
      - May need to inform the media and the public
    - Human Resources
      - May be involved if an employee is suspected of causing an incident
      - Example: disciplinary proceedings
    - Business Continuity Planning
      - Incident response policies and procedures and business continuity processes should be in sync
      - Made aware of incidents and impacts to fine-tune business impact assessments, risk assessments, and continuity of operations plans
      - May be valuable in planning responses to denial of service (DoS) conditions
    - Physical Security and Facilities Management
      - May be needed because some incidents occur through breaches of physical security or involve coordinated logical and physical attacks
      - May need access to facilities during incident handling, e.g., to acquire a compromised workstation from a locked office

- **Incident response team structure / staffing models**
  - **Team Models**
    - Central Incident Response Team
      - A single incident response team handles incidents throughout the organization
      - Effective for small organizations and organizations with minimal geographic diversity in terms of computing resources
    - Distributed Incident Response Teams
      - Multiple incident response teams, each responsible for a particular logical or physical segment of the organization
      - Effective for large organizations (e.g., one team per division) and for organizations with major computing resources at distant locations (e.g., one team per geographic region, one team per major facility)
      - Teams should be part of a single coordinated entity so that the incident response process is consistent and information is shared among teams
    - Coordinating Team
      - Provides advice to other teams without authority over those teams
      - Example: a departmentwide team may assist individual agencies’ teams
      - Not addressed in detail in this document
  - **Staffing Models**
    - Employees
      - Organization performs all incident response work, with limited technical and administrative support from contractors
    - Partially Outsourced
      - Organization outsources portions of incident response work
      - Common arrangements:
        - Outsource 24-hours-a-day, 7-days-a-week (24/7) monitoring of intrusion detection sensors, firewalls, and other security devices to an offsite managed security services provider (MSSP)
        - MSSP identifies and analyzes suspicious activity and reports each detected incident to the organization’s incident response team
        - Some organizations perform basic incident response work in-house and call on contractors to assist with handling incidents, particularly more serious or widespread incidents
    - Fully Outsourced
      - Organization completely outsources incident response work, typically to an onsite contractor
      - Most likely used when the organization needs a full-time, onsite incident response team but does not have enough available, qualified employees
      - Organization will have employees supervising and overseeing the outsourcer’s work
  - **Team model selection factors**
    - Need for 24/7 availability
      - Incident handlers can be contacted by phone
      - May require onsite presence
      - Real-time availability is best
    - Full-time versus part-time team members
      - Existing group such as the IT help desk can act as a first POC for incident reporting
      - Help desk members can be trained to perform initial investigation and data gathering and then alert the incident response team if it appears that a serious incident has occurred
    - Employee morale
      - Incident response work is very stressful
      - Segregating roles, particularly reducing administrative work team members are responsible for performing, can be a significant boost to morale
    - Cost
      - Incident response-specific costs should be included in budgets
      - Includes sufficient funding for training and maintaining skills
      - Additional costs:
        - Physical security for the team’s work areas
        - Communications mechanisms
    - Staff expertise
      - Outsourcers may possess deeper knowledge of intrusion detection, forensics, vulnerabilities, exploits, and other aspects of security
      - MSSPs may be able to correlate events among customers to identify new threats more quickly
      - Internal technical staff usually have better knowledge of the organization’s environment
  - **Outsourcing considerations**
    - Current and Future Quality of Work
      - Consider current quality and efforts to ensure future quality
      - Examples:
        - Minimizing turnover and burnout
        - Providing a solid training program for new employees
      - Assess the quality of the outsourcer’s work objectively
    - Division of Responsibilities
      - Document appropriate actions for decision points
      - Example: partially outsourced model where outsourcer provides incident data and recommendations; internal team makes operational decisions
    - Sensitive Information Revealed to the Contractor
      - Restrict access to sensitive information
      - Example: contractor may determine what user ID was used in an incident but not know what person is associated with the user ID
      - Non-disclosure agreements (NDAs) are one possible option
    - Lack of Organization-Specific Knowledge
      - Provide regularly updated documents defining:
        - What incidents the organization is concerned about
        - Which resources are critical
        - What the level of response should be under various sets of circumstances
      - Report all changes and updates made to IT infrastructure, network configuration, and systems
    - Lack of Correlation
      - Correlation among multiple data sources is very important
      - Outsourcer may require administrative privileges to critical systems and security device logs remotely over a secure channel
    - Handling Incidents at Multiple Locations
      - Consider where the outsourcer is located
      - Consider how quickly it can have an incident response team at any facility
      - Consider onsite visits
      - Consider whether there are certain facilities or areas where the outsourcer should not be permitted to work
    - Maintaining Incident Response Skills In-House
      - Organizations that completely outsource incident response should strive to maintain basic incident response skills in-house
      - Organization should be prepared to perform its own incident handling if outsourcer is unavailable
      - Technical staff must be able to understand the significance, technical implications, and impact of the outsourcer’s recommendations

- **Communication / outside parties / escalation / exception handling**
  - Organizations often need to communicate with outside parties regarding an incident, including:
    - Law enforcement
    - Media
    - Vendors
    - Victim organizations
    - Internet service providers (ISPs)
    - Other incident response teams
    - Software vendors
    - Other involved parties
  - Organizations should predetermine communication guidelines so that only appropriate information is shared with the right parties.
  - The incident response team should discuss information sharing with the organization’s public affairs office, legal department, and management before an incident occurs.
  - The team should document all contacts and communications with outside parties for liability and evidentiary purposes.
  - **The Media**
    - Establish media communications procedures that comply with the organization’s policies on media interaction and information disclosure
    - Beneficial to designate a single point of contact (POC) and at least one backup contact
    - Recommended actions:
      - Conduct training sessions on interacting with the media regarding incidents
      - Establish procedures to brief media contacts on issues and sensitivities before discussing with the media
      - Maintain a statement of the current status of the incident
      - Remind all staff of general procedures for handling media inquiries
      - Hold mock interviews and press conferences during incident handling exercises
    - Example media questions:
      - Who attacked you? Why?
      - When did it happen? How did it happen? Did this happen because you have poor security practices?
      - How widespread is this incident? What steps are you taking to determine what happened and to prevent future occurrences?
      - What is the impact of this incident? Was any personally identifiable information (PII) exposed? What is the estimated cost of this incident?
  - **Law Enforcement**
    - Multiple levels available in the United States:
      - Federal investigatory agencies (e.g., the Federal Bureau of Investigation [FBI] and the U.S. Secret Service)
      - District attorney offices
      - State law enforcement
      - Local (e.g., county) law enforcement
    - Law enforcement agencies in other countries may also be involved
    - Agencies have an Office of Inspector General (OIG) for investigation of violation of the law within each agency
    - Incident response team should become acquainted with law enforcement representatives before an incident occurs to discuss:
      - Conditions under which incidents should be reported
      - How reporting should be performed
      - What evidence should be collected
      - How evidence should be collected
    - Law enforcement should be contacted through designated individuals in a manner consistent with the requirements of the law and the organization’s procedures
    - Many organizations prefer to appoint one incident response team member as the primary POC with law enforcement
    - Do not contact multiple agencies because doing so might result in jurisdictional conflicts
    - Potential jurisdictional issues should be understood
  - **Incident Reporting Organizations / US-CERT**
    - FISMA requires Federal agencies to report incidents to US-CERT
    - US-CERT is a governmentwide incident response organization that assists Federal civilian agencies in their incident handling efforts
    - US-CERT does not replace existing agency response teams; it augments their efforts by serving as a focal point for dealing with incidents
    - US-CERT analyzes agency-provided information to identify trends and indicators of attacks
    - Each agency must designate a primary and secondary POC with US-CERT and report all incidents consistent with the agency’s incident response policy
    - Organizations should create a policy that states who is designated to report incidents and how incidents should be reported
    - Requirements, categories, and timeframes for reporting incidents to US-CERT are on the US-CERT website
    - All Federal agencies must ensure that incident response procedures adhere to US-CERT’s reporting requirements and that procedures are followed properly
    - All organizations are encouraged to report incidents to their appropriate CSIRTs
    - If an organization does not have its own CSIRT, it can report incidents to other organizations, including Information Sharing and Analysis Centers (ISACs)
    - ISAC sectors listed:
      - Communications
      - Electric Sector
      - Financial Services
      - Information Technology
      - Research and Education
  - **Other outside parties**
    - Organization’s ISP
      - May need assistance blocking a major network-based attack or tracing its origin
    - Owners of Attacking Addresses
      - If attacks originate from an external organization’s IP address space, handlers may want to talk to designated security contacts for the organization to alert them or ask them to collect evidence
      - Highly recommended to coordinate such communications with US-CERT or an ISAC
    - Software Vendors
      - May be asked about significance of log entries or known false positives for intrusion detection signatures
      - More information may be needed if a server appears compromised through an unknown software vulnerability
      - May provide information on known threats
    - Other Incident Response Teams
      - Proactively sharing information can facilitate more effective and efficient incident handling
      - Examples of groups that promote information sharing:
        - Forum of Incident Response and Security Teams (FIRST)
        - Government Forum of Incident Response and Security Teams (GFIRST)
        - Anti-Phishing Working Group (APWG)
    - Affected External Parties
      - In some jurisdictions, organizations are required to notify all parties affected by an incident
      - Preferable to notify affected external parties before the media or other external organizations do so
      - Handlers should give out only appropriate information
      - Federal agencies are required to develop and implement a breach notification policy for personally identifiable information (PII)
      - Incident handlers should understand how actions differ when a PII breach is suspected, such as notifying additional parties or notifying parties within a shorter timeframe
      - National Conference of State Legislatures has a list of state security breach notification laws

- **Incident lifecycle / phases / cadence**
  - Incident response life cycle phases:
    - Preparation
    - Detection and Analysis
    - Containment, Eradication, and Recovery
    - Post-Incident Activity
  - Preparation phase:
    - Establishing and training an incident response team
    - Acquiring necessary tools and resources
    - Selecting and implementing controls based on risk assessments to limit incidents
  - Detection and Analysis phase:
    - Analyze precursors and indicators
    - Determine whether an incident has occurred
    - Determine type, extent, and magnitude
    - Perform initial analysis to determine scope
    - Prioritize subsequent activities
  - Containment, Eradication, and Recovery phase:
    - Contain incident before it overwhelms resources or increases damage
    - Eradicate malware, breached accounts, exploited vulnerabilities, affected hosts
    - Restore systems to normal operation
    - Confirm systems function normally
    - Remediate vulnerabilities
    - Implement additional monitoring
  - Post-Incident Activity phase:
    - Lessons learned
    - Using collected incident data
    - Evidence retention
  - Incident handling checklist sequence:
    - Determine whether an incident has occurred
    - Analyze the precursors and indicators
    - Look for correlating information
    - Perform research (e.g., search engines, knowledge base)
    - As soon as the handler believes an incident has occurred, begin documenting the investigation and gathering evidence
    - Prioritize handling based on relevant factors
    - Report the incident to the appropriate internal personnel and external organizations
    - Acquire, preserve, secure, and document evidence
    - Contain the incident
    - Eradicate the incident
      - Identify and mitigate all vulnerabilities that were exploited
      - Remove malware, inappropriate materials, and other components
      - If more affected hosts are discovered, repeat Detection and Analysis steps (1.1, 1.2), then contain and eradicate for them
    - Recover from the incident
      - Return affected systems to an operationally ready state
      - Confirm affected systems are functioning normally
      - If necessary, implement additional monitoring to look for future related activity
    - Create a follow-up report
    - Hold a lessons learned meeting
  - **Cadence / frequency**
    - Incident response plan should be reviewed at least annually
    - Lessons learned meeting:
      - After a major incident
      - Optionally periodically after lesser incidents as resources permit
      - Within several days of the end of the incident
    - Lessons learned meetings can cover multiple incidents in a single meeting
    - Regular review of logs should occur
    - Follow-up reports should be kept for a period of time as specified in record retention policies
    - Evidence retention may be months or years after incident ends
    - Incident handling records under GRS 24: destroy 3 years after all necessary follow-up actions have been completed
    - For large-scale incidents, recovery may take months
    - Early remediation phases: days to weeks high value changes
    - Later phases: longer-term changes and ongoing work
  - **Communication cadence / status updates**
    - Incident response policies should include provisions concerning incident reporting, at a minimum:
      - What must be reported to whom
      - At what times
        - Initial notification
        - Regular status updates
    - The team may need to provide status updates to certain parties, even in some cases the entire organization
    - Communication methods can include:
      - Email
      - Website (internal, external, or portal)
      - Telephone calls
      - In person (e.g., daily briefings)
      - Voice mailbox greeting
      - Paper

- **Preparation / tools / resources / facilities**
  - Incident Handler Communications and Facilities:
    - Contact information for team members and others within and outside the organization (primary and backup contacts), such as law enforcement and other incident response teams; information may include phone numbers, email addresses, public encryption keys, and instructions for verifying the contact’s identity
    - On-call information for other teams within the organization, including escalation information
    - Incident reporting mechanisms:
      - Phone numbers
      - Email addresses
      - Online forms
      - Secure instant messaging systems
      - At least one mechanism should permit anonymous reporting
    - Issue tracking system for tracking incident information, status, etc.
    - Smartphones for off-hour support and onsite communications
    - Encryption software for communications among team members, within the organization and with external parties; for Federal agencies, software must use a FIPS-validated encryption algorithm
    - War room for central communication and coordination
      - If a permanent war room is not necessary or practical, create a procedure for procuring a temporary war room when needed
    - Secure storage facility for securing evidence and other sensitive materials
  - Incident Analysis Hardware and Software:
    - Digital forensic workstations and/or backup devices to create disk images, preserve log files, and save other relevant incident data
    - Laptops for analyzing data, sniffing packets, and writing reports
    - Spare workstations, servers, and networking equipment, or the virtualized equivalents
    - Blank removable media
    - Portable printer to print copies of log files and other evidence from non-networked systems
    - Packet sniffers and protocol analyzers to capture and analyze network traffic
    - Digital forensic software to analyze disk images
    - Removable media with trusted versions of programs to gather evidence from systems
    - Evidence gathering accessories:
      - Hard-bound notebooks
      - Digital cameras
      - Audio recorders
      - Chain of custody forms
      - Evidence storage bags and tags
      - Evidence tape
  - Incident Analysis Resources:
    - Port lists, including commonly used ports and Trojan horse ports
    - Documentation for OSs, applications, protocols, and intrusion detection and antivirus products
    - Network diagrams and lists of critical assets, such as database servers
    - Current baselines of expected network, system, and application activity
    - Cryptographic hashes of critical files
  - Incident Mitigation Software:
    - Access to images of clean OS and application installations for restoration and recovery purposes
  - Jump kit:
    - Portable case that contains materials needed during an investigation
    - Should be ready to go at all times
    - Typically includes:
      - Laptop loaded with appropriate software (e.g., packet sniffers, digital forensics)
      - Backup devices
      - Blank media
      - Basic networking equipment and cables
    - Team should avoid borrowing items from the jump kit
  - Each incident handler should have access to at least two computing devices
    - One investigative laptop used to perform packet sniffing, malware analysis, and all other actions that risk contaminating the laptop
    - This laptop should be scrubbed and all software reinstalled before being used for another incident
    - Incident handlers should be allowed to specify basic technical requirements for special-purpose investigative laptops whenever possible
    - A standard laptop, smart phone, or other computing device should be used for writing reports, reading email, and other duties unrelated to hands-on incident analysis
  - Exercises involving simulated incidents can be very useful for preparing staff for incident handling

- **Attack vectors / categories**
  - External/Removable Media
    - Attack executed from removable media (e.g., flash drive, CD) or a peripheral device
  - Attrition
    - Attack that employs brute force methods to compromise, degrade, or destroy systems, networks, or services
    - Example: DDoS intended to impair or deny access to a service or application; brute force attack against authentication mechanism such as passwords, CAPTCHAS, or digital signatures
  - Web
    - Attack executed from a website or web-based application
    - Example: cross-site scripting attack used to steal credentials or a redirect to a site that exploits a browser vulnerability and installs malware
  - Email
    - Attack executed via an email message or attachment
    - Example: exploit code disguised as an attached document or a link to a malicious website in the body of an email message
  - Impersonation
    - Attack involving replacement of something benign with something malicious
    - Examples: spoofing, man in the middle attacks, rogue wireless access points, and SQL injection attacks
  - Improper Usage
    - Any incident resulting from violation of an organization’s acceptable usage policies by an authorized user, excluding the above categories
    - Example: a user installs file sharing software, leading to the loss of sensitive data; or a user performs illegal activities on a system
  - Loss or Theft of Equipment
    - The loss or theft of a computing device or media used by the organization, such as a laptop, smartphone, or authentication token
  - Other
    - An attack that does not fit into any of the other categories

- **Signs of an incident / precursors / indicators**
  - Precursors are a sign that an incident may occur in the future.
  - Indicators are a sign that an incident may have occurred or may be occurring now.
  - Most attacks do not have identifiable or detectable precursors from the target’s perspective.
  - Examples of precursors:
    - Web server log entries that show the usage of a vulnerability scanner
    - An announcement of a new exploit that targets a vulnerability of the organization’s mail server
    - A threat from a group stating that the group will attack the organization
  - Examples of indicators:
    - A network intrusion detection sensor alerts when a buffer overflow attempt occurs against a database server
    - Antivirus software alerts when it detects that a host is infected with malware
    - A system administrator sees a filename with unusual characters
    - A host records an auditing configuration change in its log
    - An application logs multiple failed login attempts from an unfamiliar remote system
    - An email administrator sees a large number of bounced emails with suspicious content
    - A network administrator notices an unusual deviation from typical network traffic flows

- **Sources of precursors and indicators / fields captured**
  - **Alerts**
    - IDPSs
      - Record:
        - Date and time the attack was detected
        - Type of attack
        - Source and destination IP addresses
        - Username, if applicable and known
      - Use attack signatures
      - Must be kept up to date
      - Produce false positives
    - SIEMs
      - Generate alerts based on analysis of log data
    - Antivirus and antispam software
      - Antivirus detects various forms of malware, generates alerts, and prevents malware from infecting hosts
      - Antispam detects spam and prevents it from reaching users’ mailboxes
      - Spam may contain malware, phishing attacks, and other malicious content
    - File integrity checking software
      - Detects changes made to important files during incidents
      - Uses a hashing algorithm to obtain a cryptographic checksum for each designated file
      - By regularly recalculating checksums and comparing them with previous values, changes to files can be detected
    - Third-party monitoring services
      - Fraud detection services notify an organization if its IP addresses, domain names, etc. are associated with current incident activity involving other organizations
      - Free real-time blacklists with similar information
      - CSIRC notification list
  - **Logs**
    - Operating system, service and application logs
      - Particularly audit-related data
      - Frequently of great value when an incident occurs
      - Record which accounts were accessed and what actions were performed
      - Require a baseline level of logging on all systems and a higher baseline level on critical systems
      - Logs can be used for analysis by correlating event information
    - Network device logs
      - Firewalls and routers usually log blocked connection attempts
      - Valuable in identifying network trends and correlating events detected by other devices
    - Network flows
      - Communication session occurring between hosts
      - Can be used to find anomalous network activity caused by malware, data exfiltration, and other malicious acts
      - Standards:
        - NetFlow
        - sFlow
        - IPFIX
  - **Publicly available information**
    - Information on new vulnerabilities and exploits
      - NVD contains information on vulnerabilities
      - US-CERT periodically provides threat update information through briefings, web postings, and mailing lists
      - CERT®/CC periodically provides threat update information through briefings, web postings, and mailing lists
  - **People**
    - People from within the organization
      - Users, system administrators, network administrators, security staff, and others may report signs of incidents
      - Important to validate all such reports
      - One approach: ask people how confident they are of the accuracy of the information
      - Recording this estimate along with the information provided can help during incident analysis
    - People from other organizations
      - Reports of incidents that originate externally should be taken seriously
      - External users may report indicators such as a defaced web page or an unavailable service
      - Other incident response teams may also report incidents
      - Need mechanisms for external parties to report indicators and for trained staff to monitor those mechanisms carefully
      - Example: phone number and email address configured to forward messages to the help desk

- **Incident analysis / documentation / evidence / templates / fields captured**
  - Incident response team should work quickly to analyze and validate each incident, following a pre-defined process and documenting each step taken.
  - Initial analysis should determine:
    - Incident’s scope
    - Which networks, systems, or applications are affected
    - Who or what originated the incident
    - How the incident is occurring
      - What tools or attack methods are being used
      - What vulnerabilities are being exploited
  - Recommendations for making incident analysis easier and more effective:
    - Profile networks and systems
      - Measuring characteristics of expected activity
      - Examples: file integrity checking software; monitoring network bandwidth usage to determine average and peak usage levels
    - Understand normal behaviors
      - Study networks, systems, and applications to understand normal behavior
      - Review log entries and security alerts
      - Conduct frequent log reviews
      - Review gives analyst indication of reliability of each source
    - Create a Log Retention Policy
      - Specifies how long log data should be maintained
      - Older log entries may show reconnaissance activity or previous instances of similar attacks
      - Length of time depends on organization’s data retention policies and volume of data
    - Perform Event Correlation
      - Correlate events among multiple indicator sources
      - Example: firewall log has source IP address; application log contains username; host logs can determine whether attack was successful
    - Keep All Host Clocks Synchronized
      - Use Network Time Protocol (NTP)
      - Consistent timestamps in logs are preferable
    - Maintain and Use a Knowledge Base of Information
      - Should include information needed for quick reference during incident analysis
      - Can be text documents, spreadsheets, and relatively simple databases
      - Should contain explanations of the significance and validity of precursors and indicators, such as:
        - IDPS alerts
        - Operating system log entries
        - Application error codes
    - Use Internet Search Engines for Research
      - Separate workstations should be used for research to minimize risk
    - Run Packet Sniffers to Collect Additional Data
      - Can capture network traffic
      - Configure sniffer to record traffic that matches specified criteria
      - Some organizations may require permission before using packet sniffers
    - Filter the Data
      - Investigate most suspicious activity at minimum
      - One strategy is to filter out categories of indicators that tend to be insignificant
      - Another strategy is to show only categories of highest significance
    - Seek Assistance from Others
      - Internal resources:
        - Information security staff
      - External resources:
        - US-CERT
        - Other CSIRTs
        - Contractors with incident response expertise
  - Incident documentation requirements
    - Suspected incident: immediately start recording all facts regarding the incident
    - Logbook is an effective and simple medium
    - Alternatives:
      - Laptops
      - Audio recorders
      - Digital cameras
    - Document every step taken from the time the incident was detected to its final resolution
    - Every document regarding the incident should be dated and signed by the incident handler
    - Work in teams of at least two whenever possible:
      - One person can record and log events
      - The other person performs technical tasks
    - Incident communications and documents should be encrypted or otherwise protected so only authorized personnel can read them
  - Issue tracking system should contain:
    - Current status of the incident
      - new
      - in progress
      - forwarded for investigation
      - resolved
    - Summary of the incident
    - Indicators related to the incident
    - Other incidents related to this incident
    - Actions taken by all incident handlers on this incident
    - Chain of custody, if applicable
    - Impact assessments related to the incident
    - Contact information for other involved parties
      - system owners
      - system administrators
    - A list of evidence gathered during the incident investigation
    - Comments from incident handlers
    - Next steps to be taken
      - rebuild the host
      - upgrade an application
  - Incident response team should safeguard incident data and restrict access to it
    - Example: only authorized personnel should have access to the incident database

- **Incident prioritization / severity / SLA / escalation**
  - Prioritizing the handling of the incident is the most critical decision point in the incident handling process.
  - Incidents should not be handled on a first-come, first-served basis.
  - Prioritize based on:
    - Functional Impact of the Incident
      - Current and likely future negative impact to business functions
    - Information Impact of the Incident
      - Effect on confidentiality, integrity, and availability of the organization’s information
    - Recoverability from the Incident
      - Time and types of resources required to recover
  - Business impact = combining functional impact and information impact
  - Functional Impact Categories
    - None: No effect to the organization’s ability to provide all services to all users
    - Low: Minimal effect; the organization can still provide all critical services to all users but has lost efficiency
    - Medium: Organization has lost the ability to provide a critical service to a subset of system users
    - High: Organization is no longer able to provide some critical services to any users
  - Information Impact Categories
    - None: No information was exfiltrated, changed, deleted, or otherwise compromised
    - Privacy Breach: Sensitive personally identifiable information (PII) of taxpayers, employees, beneficiaries, etc. was accessed or exfiltrated
    - Proprietary Breach: Unclassified proprietary information, such as protected critical infrastructure information (PCII), was accessed or exfiltrated
    - Integrity Loss: Sensitive or proprietary information was changed or deleted
  - Recoverability Effort Categories
    - Regular: Time to recovery is predictable with existing resources
    - Supplemented: Time to recovery is predictable with additional resources
    - Extended: Time to recovery is unpredictable; additional resources and outside help are needed
    - Not Recoverable: Recovery from the incident is not possible (e.g., sensitive data exfiltrated and posted publicly); launch investigation
  - Escalation process
    - Should state how long a person should wait for a response and what to do if no response occurs
    - Example:
      - duplicate the initial contact
      - after perhaps 15 minutes, escalate to incident response team manager
      - if no response, escalate again to higher level of management
      - repeat until someone responds
  - Response time depends on:
    - Type of incident
    - Criticality of the resources and data that are affected
    - Severity of the incident
    - Existing Service Level Agreements (SLA) for affected resources
    - Time and day of the week
    - Other incidents that the team is handling
  - Highest priority is handling incidents likely to cause the most damage to the organization or to other organizations

- **Incident reporting / notification fields**
  - Incident response policies should include provisions concerning incident reporting
    - At a minimum:
      - What must be reported to whom
      - At what times
        - Initial notification
        - Regular status updates
  - Parties typically notified:
    - CIO
    - Head of information security
    - Local information security officer
    - Other incident response teams within the organization
    - External incident response teams, if appropriate
    - System owner
    - Human resources
    - Public affairs
    - Legal department
    - US-CERT
    - Law enforcement, if appropriate
  - Communication methods:
    - Email
    - Website (internal, external, or portal)
    - Telephone calls
    - In person
    - Voice mailbox greeting
    - Paper

- **Containment / eradication / recovery**
  - Containment
    - Important before an incident overwhelms resources or increases damage
    - Provides time for developing a tailored remediation strategy
    - Essential part of containment is decision-making:
      - Shut down a system
      - Disconnect it from a network
      - Disable certain functions
    - Organizations should define acceptable risks in dealing with incidents and develop strategies accordingly
    - Separate containment strategies should be created for each major incident type
    - Criteria for determining appropriate strategy:
      - Potential damage to and theft of resources
      - Need for evidence preservation
      - Service availability
      - Time and resources needed to implement the strategy
      - Effectiveness of the strategy
      - Duration of the solution
        - emergency workaround to be removed in four hours
        - temporary workaround to be removed in two weeks
        - permanent solution
    - Sandbox redirection may be used in certain cases to monitor attacker activity and gather additional evidence
      - Discuss with legal department to determine if feasible
    - Delayed containment is dangerous because attacker could escalate unauthorized access or compromise other systems
    - Some attacks may cause additional damage when contained
      - Example: malicious process may overwrite or encrypt all data on host’s hard drive when subsequent pings fail after disconnection
  - Evidence gathering and handling
    - Primary reason: resolve the incident
    - May also be needed for legal proceedings
    - Evidence, including compromised systems, should be preserved and clearly documented
    - Evidence should be collected according to procedures that meet all applicable laws and regulations
    - Evidence should be accounted for at all times
    - Chain of custody forms should detail each transfer and include each party’s signature
    - Detailed log should be kept for all evidence including:
      - Identifying information
        - location
        - serial number
        - model number
        - hostname
        - media access control (MAC) addresses
        - IP addresses of a computer
      - Name, title, and phone number of each individual who collected or handled the evidence during the investigation
      - Time and date, including time zone, of each occurrence of evidence handling
      - Locations where the evidence was stored
    - Acquire evidence from a system of interest as soon as one suspects that an incident may have occurred
    - Initial system snapshot may do more good than most other actions
    - Better to get a snapshot of the system as-is rather than after others have altered it
    - Users and system administrators should be made aware of steps that they should take to preserve evidence
  - Identifying attacking hosts
    - Validate the attacking host’s IP address
    - Research the attacking host through search engines
    - Use incident databases
    - Monitor possible attacker communication channels
      - Example: IRC
    - Treat such information only as a potential lead, not as fact
  - Eradication and recovery
    - After containment, eradication may be necessary to eliminate components of the incident, such as deleting malware and disabling breached user accounts, and identifying and mitigating all vulnerabilities that were exploited
    - During eradication, identify all affected hosts within the organization so that they can be remediated
    - In recovery, administrators restore systems to normal operation, confirm systems are functioning normally, and remediate vulnerabilities if applicable
    - Recovery actions may include:
      - Restoring systems from clean backups
      - Rebuilding systems from scratch
      - Replacing compromised files with clean versions
      - Installing patches
      - Changing passwords
      - Tightening network perimeter security
        - firewall rulesets
        - boundary router access control lists
    - Higher levels of system logging or network monitoring are often part of recovery
    - Eradication and recovery should be done in a phased approach so remediation steps are prioritized
    - For large-scale incidents, recovery may take months
    - Early phases should increase overall security with relatively quick high value changes
    - Later phases should focus on longer-term changes and ongoing work

- **Lessons learned / audit / remediation records / POA&M-like records**
  - Lessons learned meeting
    - Hold after a major incident
    - Optionally periodically for lesser incidents as time and resources permit
    - Held within several days of the end of the incident
    - Questions to answer:
      - Exactly what happened, and at what times?
      - How well did staff and management perform in dealing with the incident?
      - Were the documented procedures followed?
      - Were they adequate?
      - What information was needed sooner?
      - Were any steps or actions taken that might have inhibited the recovery?
      - What would the staff and management do differently the next time a similar incident occurs?
      - How could information sharing with other organizations have been improved?
      - What corrective actions can prevent similar incidents in the future?
      - What precursors or indicators should be watched for in the future to detect similar incidents?
      - What additional tools or resources are needed to detect, analyze, and mitigate future incidents?
    - Multiple incidents can be covered in a single lessons learned meeting
    - Major points of agreement and action items should be documented and communicated to parties who could not attend
    - Reports from lessons learned meetings are good material for training new team members
    - Updating incident response policies and procedures is another important part
    - Incident response team should review all related documentation and procedures for handling incidents at designated intervals
  - Follow-up report
    - Create for each incident
    - Valuable for future use
    - Provides reference for handling similar incidents
    - Formal chronology of events, including timestamped information such as log data from systems, is important for legal reasons
    - Monetary estimate of amount of damage caused is important
    - May become basis for subsequent prosecution activity by entities such as the U.S. Attorney General’s office
    - Should be kept for a period of time as specified in record retention policies
  - Using collected incident data
    - Objective and subjective data from lessons learned should be produced
    - Data may be used to justify additional funding of the incident response team
    - Study of incident characteristics may indicate systemic security weaknesses and threats, as well as changes in incident trends
    - Data can be put back into the risk assessment process, leading to additional controls
    - Data can be used to measure success of incident response team
    - Data can be used to determine if a change to incident response capabilities causes corresponding change in team performance
    - Required incident reporting information must be collected to meet requirements
    - Organizations should focus on collecting actionable data
    - Possible metrics:
      - Number of Incidents Handled
      - Time Per Incident
        - Total amount of labor spent
        - Elapsed time from beginning of incident to discovery
        - Elapsed time to initial impact assessment
        - Elapsed time to each stage of incident handling process
          - containment
          - recovery
        - Time to respond to initial report
        - Time to report incident to management and, if necessary, appropriate external entities
      - Objective Assessment of Each Incident
        - Reviewing logs, forms, reports, and other incident documentation for adherence to established policies and procedures
        - Identifying which precursors and indicators were recorded
        - Determining if the incident caused damage before it was detected
        - Determining if the actual cause was identified, and identifying:
          - vector of attack
          - vulnerabilities exploited
          - characteristics of targeted or victimized systems, networks, and applications
        - Determining if the incident is a recurrence of a previous incident
        - Calculating estimated monetary damage
        - Measuring the difference between the initial impact assessment and the final impact assessment
        - Identifying which measures, if any, could have prevented the incident
      - Subjective Assessment of Each Incident
        - Incident response team members may assess their own performance, other team members, and the entire team
        - Resource owner may assess whether incident was handled efficiently and if outcome was satisfactory
    - Organizations may periodically audit their incident response programs
      - At a minimum, an incident response audit should evaluate:
        - Incident response policies, plans, and procedures
        - Tools and resources
        - Team model and structure
        - Incident handler training and education
        - Incident documentation and reports
        - Measures of success discussed earlier in this section
  - Evidence retention
    - Policy should define how long evidence from an incident should be retained
    - Most organizations choose to retain all evidence for months or years after the incident ends
    - Factors:
      - Prosecution
        - If attacker may be prosecuted, evidence may need to be retained until all legal actions have been completed
        - Evidence that seems insignificant now may become more important in the future
      - Data retention
        - Example: email messages retained only 180 days
        - Disk image containing thousands of emails may not be kept for more than 180 days unless necessary
        - GRS 24 specifies that incident handling records should be kept for three years
      - Cost
        - Original hardware, hard drives, compromised systems, hard drives and removable media used to hold disk images are individually inexpensive
        - Storing many such components for years can be substantial
        - Functional computers needed to use stored hardware and media must also be retained

- **Incident-related data elements / template headings / fields captured**
  - Organizations should identify a standard set of incident-related data elements to be collected for each incident.
  - Two sets:
    - Basic elements collected when incident is reported
    - Additional elements collected by incident handlers during response
  - These form basis for the incident reporting database
  - **Basic Data Elements**
    - Contact Information for the Incident Reporter and Handler
      - Name
      - Role
      - Organizational unit (e.g., agency, department, division, team) and affiliation
      - Email address
      - Phone number
      - Location (e.g., mailing address, office room number)
    - Incident Details
      - Status change date/timestamps (including time zone): when the incident started, when the incident was discovered/detected, when the incident was reported, when the incident was resolved/ended, etc.
      - Physical location of the incident (e.g., city, state)
      - Current status of the incident (e.g., ongoing attack)
      - Source/cause of the incident (if known), including hostnames and IP addresses
      - Description of the incident (e.g., how it was detected, what occurred)
      - Description of affected resources (e.g., networks, hosts, applications, data), including systems’ hostnames, IP addresses, and function
      - If known, incident category, vectors of attack associated with the incident, and indicators related to the incident (traffic patterns, registry keys, etc.)
      - Prioritization factors (functional impact, information impact, recoverability, etc.)
      - Mitigating factors (e.g., stolen laptop containing sensitive data was using full disk encryption)
      - Response actions performed (e.g., shut off host, disconnected host from network)
      - Other organizations contacted (e.g., software vendor)
    - General Comments
  - **Incident Handler Data Elements**
    - Current Status of the Incident Response
    - Summary of the Incident
    - Incident Handling Actions
      - Log of actions taken by all handlers
      - Contact information for all involved parties
      - List of evidence gathered
    - Incident Handler Comments
    - Cause of the Incident (e.g., misconfigured application, unpatched host)
    - Cost of the Incident
    - Business Impact of the Incident

- **Training / exercises / tabletop**
  - Appendix A incident handling scenarios are for tabletop discussions
  - Incident handling scenarios are an inexpensive and effective way to build incident response skills and identify potential issues with incident response processes
  - Team discusses each question and determines the most likely answer
  - Goal is to determine what the team would really do and compare that with policies, procedures, and generally recommended practices
  - Organizations are strongly encouraged to adapt these questions and scenarios for use in their own incident response exercises
  - Exercises involving simulated incidents can also be very useful for preparing staff for incident handling

- **Crisis handling steps**
  - This is a list of the major steps to be performed when a technical professional believes that a serious incident has occurred and the organization does not have an incident response capability available.
  - Steps:
    1. Document everything
       - Every action performed
       - Every piece of evidence
       - Every conversation with users, system owners, and others regarding the incident
    2. Find a coworker who can provide assistance
       - Handling will be much easier if two or more people work together
       - One person can perform actions while the other documents them
    3. Analyze the evidence to confirm that an incident has occurred
       - Perform additional research as necessary
       - Reach out to other technical professionals within the organization for additional help
    4. Notify the appropriate people within the organization
       - CIO
       - Head of information security
       - Local security manager
       - Use discretion when discussing details
       - Tell only the people who need to know
       - Use communication mechanisms that are reasonably secure
       - If attacker has compromised email services, do not send emails about the incident
    5. Notify US-CERT and/or other external organizations for assistance in dealing with the incident
    6. Stop the incident if it is still in progress
       - Disconnect affected systems from the network
       - Modify firewall and router configurations to stop network traffic that is part of an incident, such as a DoS attack
    7. Preserve evidence from the incident
       - Make backups, preferably disk image backups, not file system backups, of affected systems
       - Make copies of log files that contain evidence related to the incident
    8. Wipe out all effects of the incident
       - Malware infections
       - Inappropriate materials (e.g., pirated software)
       - Trojan horse files
       - Any other changes made to systems by incidents
       - If a system has been fully compromised, rebuild it from scratch or restore it from a known good backup
    9. Identify and mitigate all vulnerabilities that were exploited
       - Identify vulnerabilities in operating systems or applications
       - Eliminate or otherwise mitigate them so the incident does not recur
    10. Confirm that operations have been restored to normal
       - Make sure that data, applications, and other services affected by the incident have been returned to normal operations
    11. Create a final report
       - Detail the incident handling process
       - Provide an executive summary of what happened and how a formal incident response capability would have helped to handle the situation, mitigate the risk, and limit the damage more quickly
