| RACI / role responsibilities | - SREs diagnose, mitigate, fix, or escalate incidents as needed.<br>- SRE teams mitigate incidents, repair production problems, and automate operational tasks.<br>- On-call engineers are human points of contact for escalations.<br>- On-call responder assesses impact to users when an event pages (P1).<br>- For Sev 1 incidents, the incident manager is paged, a scribe and communications lead is elected, and communication channels open.<br>- For Sev 2 or Sev 3, the on-call responder handles the incident lifecycle, including an abbreviated postmortem for incident review.<br>- SREs own the site operation.<br>- SREs define a shared roadmap for reliability, drive the full resolution of issues, and maintain monitoring rules.<br>- Feature developers are necessary collaborators but do not own these endeavors.<br>- Action items are assigned only to the five DevOps engineers—who are now SREs.<br>- SREs work with subject experts, many of them feature developers, to accomplish tasks.<br>- SREs send code reviews to the subject experts.<br>- The technical program manager and the people manager of the team approached senior management with a project proposal.<br>- SRE managers and tech leads should make sure that production bugs are promptly dealt with, and escalate to the developer team decision makers when necessary.<br>- When introducing a new paging alert, anyone on the team can write a new alert, but the whole team reviews proposed alert additions and can suggest alternatives.<br>- On-call engineers should update the playbook with fresh information when the corresponding page fires.<br>- Team members should follow up with each other when handoffs don’t adhere to expectations. |
| Task / ticket / playbook templates | - On-call playbooks contain high-level instructions on how to respond to automated alerts.<br>- Playbooks explain the severity and impact of the alert.<br>- Playbooks include debugging suggestions and possible actions to take to mitigate impact and fully resolve the alert.<br>- Whenever an alert is created, a corresponding playbook entry is usually created.<br>- Each alert should have a corresponding playbook entry.<br>- Any P1 or P2 event has an incident ticket attached to it.<br>- The ticket is used for event triage and tracking remediation actions, as well as for SLO impact, number of occurrences, and postmortem doc links, where applicable.<br>- File a placeholder bug for each paging alert in the bug tracking system.<br>- Create a link between the paging alerts from the monitoring system and the relevant bug in the bug tracking system.<br>- The event triage/remediation/postmortem workflow references incident tickets. |
| Escalation / exception handling | - The guidelines specified when to escalate to others.<br>- At three months, the Mountain View team became the primary on-call, with the Kirkland SREs as backup.<br>- The team could easily escalate to the Kirkland SREs if needed.<br>- The on-call engineer is never alone; ask a colleague to review findings.<br>- For Sev 1 incidents, the incident manager is paged, a scribe and communications lead is elected, and communication channels open.<br>- For events rating Sev 2 or Sev 3, the on-call responder handles the incident lifecycle, including an abbreviated postmortem.<br>- If a page is serious enough to warrant a postmortem, follow the methodology to catalog and track follow-up action items.<br>- If the problem cannot be identified, add monitoring and/or logging to help find the root cause the next time it occurs.<br>- If at all possible, avoid changes that can’t be rolled back, such as API-incompatible changes and lockstep releases.<br>- Use feature isolation to disable feature X without affecting feature Y.<br>- Drain requests away from elements of the system exhibiting the bug.<br>- Relaxing alert thresholds is rarely an appropriate response to being paged when fully subscribing to SLO-based and symptom-based alerting.<br>- New paging alerts should be explicitly approved or disallowed as a team.<br>- If introducing a new paging alert causes the service to exceed its paging budget, the stability of the system needs additional attention.<br>- Nonurgent swaps are typically best-effort.<br>- Teams with a strict pager response SLO need commute coverage.<br>- The policy for swaps should be documented.<br>- Peer review of schedule changes provides a good tradeoff between safety and flexibility.<br>- A team member can temporarily leave the rotation for long-term breaks.<br>- Teams should allow on-callers to temporarily leave the rotation because of changes in personal circumstances or burnout. |
| Cadence / frequency | - Set period of being on-call.<br>- Typical shift length examples: per day or per week.<br>- One shift is 12 hours.<br>- Google’s maximum of two distinct incidents per 12-hour shift.<br>- We target a maximum of two incidents per on-call shift.<br>- The Mountain View team reviewed a checklist during onboarding before going on-call.<br>- After two months, Sara, Mike, and the SRE transfer shadowed the on-call shifts of the outgoing Kirkland SRE team.<br>- At three months, they became the primary on-call.<br>- Weekly production meetings.<br>- Daily on-call handoffs.<br>- Daily stand-up with morning incident review.<br>- Monthly service review meeting.<br>- A week of testing is probably about right for new alerts, depending on the alert and the system.<br>- A 21-day trailing average was found to be useful for pager load trends.<br>- Google has hundreds of on-call rotations across services of all sizes.<br>- The team in overload had regularly been receiving five paging incidents per shift for the past year.<br>- Fully one-third of shifts were exceeding their pager budget.<br>- The new SRE team’s monitoring improvements targeted 99.98% availability, or roughly 26 minutes of downtime per quarter.<br>- For 99.99% availability, approximately 15 minutes of error budget per quarter.<br>- For 99.999% availability, error budget of 80 seconds per quarter.<br>- New SRE hires were not expected to be ready for on-call before three to nine months.<br>- The team held lab sessions and “deep dives” during ramp-up.<br>- The team still held “Wheel of Misfortune” sessions after ramp-up, rotating through each team member as the session leader. |
